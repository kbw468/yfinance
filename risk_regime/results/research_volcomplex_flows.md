# Deep research: vol-complex signals with out-of-sample evidence, and flow-driven regime mechanics 2018-2026

**Question.** Two linked questions about US equity-index (S&P 500) regime signals, daily data, 2000-2026, with specific performance numbers and validation protocols wherever available. (1) VOL-COMPLEX SIGNALS WITH OUT-OF-SAMPLE EVIDENCE: which signals built from the volatility complex have documented out-of-sample power for (a) equity drawdowns or crashes within days to a few months, (b) rallies or rebounds, and (c) realized-volatility expansion? Cover the VIX level and its percentile, VIX term structure (VIX/VIX3M, VIX9D/VIX, VIX1D, VIX6M, futures contango/backwardation), VVIX and the VVIX/VIX ratio, MOVE and cross-asset vol (GVZ, OVX, VXN), SKEW, the variance risk premium (implied minus realized), realized-vol estimators (close-to-close, Parkinson, Garman-Klass) and their ratios, cross-sectional dispersion and average pairwise correlation, volume and the volatility of volume, and credit-market signals (HYG, LQD realized vol, signed volume). For each: the published or practitioner result, the horizon, the metric (AUC, hit rate, Sharpe or drawdown change), whether it was walk-forward or in-sample, and which results later failed or were shown to be artifacts. Note explicitly any evidence that vol LEVEL beats vol RATE-OF-CHANGE (or vice versa) for drawdown prediction at monthly horizons, and evidence that high-vol states are two-tailed (both deeper drawdowns and larger rallies). (2) FLOW-DRIVEN REGIME MECHANICS 2018-2026: how volatility-targeting and vol-control funds, risk-parity, CTAs/trend followers, option dealer gamma positioning (0DTE growth, vanna/charm flows) and leveraged-ETF rebalancing change the speed and shape of selloffs and rebounds (Feb 2018, Dec 2018, Mar 2020, 2022, Aug 2024, Apr 2025), with estimates of flow sizes and whether any public signal (e.g., realized-vol crosses at 20%/25%, VIX term-structure inversion, dealer gamma estimates) tracks these flows out of sample. Prefer academic sources (Journal of Finance, JFE, RFS, Quantitative Finance, Journal of Financial Markets, SSRN working papers), central-bank and BIS research, and practitioner research from banks and vol-focused shops; US market; cite specific papers, authors, years and numbers.

**Summary.** Across the 23 verified claims, the only volatility-complex signals with rigorously documented power for S&P 500 index returns are risk-premium spreads, not vol levels: the variance risk premium (VIX-squared minus realized variance) predicts 1-6 month excess returns with a hump peaking at 3-4 months (adj. R-squared 6.8% at 3 months over 1990-2007; 13.0% over 2000-2011), but these are full-sample regressions, standard Newey-West t-statistics are oversized under the null (simulated 95th percentiles 2.3-3.5 vs 1.645), and a genuine walk-forward test of the traditional VRP forecast gives OOS R-squared of -0.8% to +5.2%, none significant, with the best case driven by two 2008 months; only a leverage-effect-conditioned variant (Pyun 2019) reaches 5-9% OOS R-squared, in a single unreplicated paper. The VIX or realized-variance LEVEL alone has no mean-return predictive power at monthly to annual horizons; the VIX term-structure slope and VVIX forecast returns on volatility assets (VIX futures, straddles, OTM puts), not equity returns; an inverted VIX curve is a variance-risk-premium signal (long volatility is paid in the bottom slope quintile) rather than a forecast that the VIX will keep rising; and the VIX-futures premium falls, not rises, as risk rises, with a one-SD fall predicting a roughly 3-point rise in next-month S&P 500 realized volatility. The CBOE-style SKEW index is a weak, fragile and often-misread drawdown signal (pseudo-R-squared 2-8%, OOS R-squared 0.6-2.3% for 5% monthly drops; negatively correlated with VIX), with the only robust content being a put-only 'complacency' component. Nothing on flow-driven regime mechanics (vol-control, risk parity, CTAs, dealer gamma/0DTE, leveraged ETFs, episode flow sizes) and nothing on MOVE, cross-asset vol, realized-vol estimator ratios, dispersion, volume or credit signals survived verification, so part (2) of the question and most of the signal catalogue in part (1) remain unanswered by this evidence set; every verified sample ends between 2011 and 2017, before the 2018-2026 flow regime.

## Findings

### In-sample, the variance risk premium (VIX-squared minus realized variance) positively predicts S&P 500 excess returns at 1-6 month horizons with a hump-shaped explanatory-power profile peaking at 3-4 months; it is the best-documented vol-complex predictor of index returns, but all headline numbers are full-sample OLS with overlapping observations, not walk-forward, and they measure mean-return predictability rather than crash probability or AUC.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 (both claims)

Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (RFS 2009), monthly Jan 1990-Dec 2007, RV from 78 five-minute returns plus overnight: adj. R-squared 1.07% at 1 month (Hodrick t 1.76), 6.82% at 3 months (t 2.86), 5.42% at 6 months (t 2.15), 2.30% at 9 months (t 1.36), 1.23% at 12 months (t 1.00), 0.05% at 18 months, -0.50% at 24 months; slopes fall from 0.47 (quarterly) to 0.01 (24 months); high VRP predicts high future returns. Bollerslev, Marrone, Xu & Zhou (JFQA 2014), monthly Jan 2000-Dec 2011 (includes 2008-2011): slope 0.50 (t_NW 4.17, adj. R-squared 8.89%) at 1 month; 0.37 (t 6.39, 13.03%) at 3 months; 12.83% at 4 months; 0.10% at 9 and -0.53% at 12 months; the 13.03% exceeds the pre-2008 BTZ and Drechsler-Yaron (2011) results. Verifiers note Drechsler-Yaron (RFS 2011) and Bekaert-Hoerova (J. Econometrics 2014) replicate the positive sign and 3-6 month peak. Both papers verified number-for-number against primary PDFs (claims 10, 13 merged).

Sources: https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Published_Papers/rfs_09.pdf, https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Published_Papers/jfqa_14.pdf

### The LEVEL of implied variance (VIX-squared) or of realized variance by itself has no statistically significant predictive power for S&P 500 returns at monthly, quarterly or annual horizons (1990-2007); only the implied-minus-realized spread does, and that result depends critically on using model-free VIX-squared and high-frequency realized variance rather than Black-Scholes ATM implied variance and daily close-to-close realized variance.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 (both claims)

BTZ 2009 Tables 3-6: quarterly IV alone t = 1.41 (adj. R-squared 2.49%), RV alone t = 0.00 (-0.47%); monthly IV t = 1.30 (0.57%), RV t = 0.41 (-0.34%); annual IV t = -0.21 (-0.37%), RV t = -1.20 (2.89%); joint quarterly regression VRP t = 2.91 while RV t = -0.41, i.e. the data attribute equal and opposite effects to IV and RV. Measurement: Black-Scholes IV minus daily RV cuts the quarterly adj. R-squared from 6.82% to 2.16% (t 1.72); BS IV with high-frequency RV gives t 1.85, 3.30%; model-free IV with daily RV gives t 2.34, 4.45%; volatility (square-root) form barely changes results (t 2.75, R-squared 6.51%). Nuance: VRP alone is also insignificant at the annual horizon (t 1.00) and becomes significant only alongside P/E, term spread and RREL. Cross-asset corroboration: Cheng (RFS 2019) finds the VIX level adds nothing to VIX-futures return predictability once the premium is included (coefficient 0.003, s.e. 0.002, premium stays 1.02). Single primary source for the equity-return result, consistent with the broader null risk-return literature; in-sample only (claims 14, 15 merged).

Sources: https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Published_Papers/rfs_09.pdf, https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/682f3c99-5d7d-4dee-b4b4-dafa83785523/download

### The in-sample VRP evidence materially overstates real-time usefulness: the traditional rolling-regression VRP forecast has no statistically significant out-of-sample power for one-month S&P 500 returns over 1993-2016 (and 1990-2021 in an independent re-test), the Newey-West t-statistics standard in this literature are oversized under the null of no predictability, and the 2014 'out-of-sample' confirmation is in fact full-sample OLS on an extended 2000-2011 window with no recursive forecast, OOS R-squared, Clark-West test or Sharpe ratio reported.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 (all three claims)

Pyun (JFE 2019) Table 2 Panel A: 10-year rolling predictive regression, 288 months 1993-2016, benchmark trailing 10-year mean; OOS R-squared -0.8% to +5.2% across three VRP measures; Giacomini-White Wald p-values 0.22-0.95, none significant at 10%. The gap between 0.2% (monthly-average VRP) and 5.2% (end-of-month parametric VRP) is due to Sept 2008 (negative VRP followed by -17.2%) and Oct 2008 (VRP spike followed by -7.8%); removing them narrows the range to 1.6%-3.6% (verifier: 'largely an artifact' slightly overstates since up to 3.6% remains, though still insignificant). Robust to 5/7-year windows, expanding window, WLS (p > 0.14). Independent corroboration cited by verifier: Goyal, Welch & Zafirov (RFS 2024) extend the BTZ vrp to 1990-2021, IS t 0.72, OOS R-squared 0.21% insignificant, and 'dismiss vrp as a useful predictor'. BMXZ 2014 Monte Carlo (2,000 VAR(1)-GARCH(1,1)-DCC bootstraps fit to Feb 1996-Dec 2007 daily data, 149 months): 95th percentile of t_NW under the null 2.26 at 1 month rising to 3.51 at 12 months (nominal 1.645); null 95th percentile of adj. R-squared 3.0% (1m), 4.8%-6.3% (2-4m), 8.7% (12m); on 1996-2007 actual t_NW exceeds simulated critical values only at 2-3 months and Hodrick t-stats never do; authors keep NW on size-adjusted power grounds (0.89 vs 0.81 at 1 month) and conclude finite-sample bias cannot fully explain the 2-4 month result. BMXZ themselves put 'out-of-sample' in scare quotes; a full-text grep finds no recursive/expanding/Clark-West/Sharpe evaluation (claims 3, 11, 12 merged).

Sources: https://sjpyun.github.io/VRPTS.pdf, https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Published_Papers/jfqa_14.pdf

### Walk-forward VRP forecasting works only when it conditions on the strength of the leverage effect: replacing the rolling predictive slope with the negative of last month's contemporaneous return-on-variance-innovation beta times the VRP yields OOS R-squared of 5.4%-8.5% (1993-2016) and a Sharpe gain of about 0.13-0.15 (+21%), and the traditional VRP forecast itself has OOS R-squared of 6.8%-20.7% in months when returns and variance co-move strongly but negative (-13.5% to 0%) when they do not. This is a regime-dependent, single-paper result with no post-2016 replication, no transaction costs and no significance test on the Sharpe gains.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 (both claims)

Pyun (JFE 2019) Table 2 Panel B-1 (no intercept, 1-month beta, benchmark trailing 10-year mean): OOS R-squared 6.5%/5.4% (non-parametric VRP, WLS/OLS beta), 7.9%/6.8% (parametric), 8.4%/8.5% (end-of-month parametric); Giacomini-White p 0.005-0.055, all < 0.10, five of six < 0.05. Mean-variance strategy (gamma 3, weight capped at 0-200%): Sharpe +0.129 to +0.151 vs benchmark 0.632, certainty equivalent +3.9% to 5.8%/yr; traditional VRP gain at most 0.04 (verifier notes Table 5 shows +0.290 for one parametric spec, inconsistent with the text). Table 3 (state classified in real time from trailing terciles of corr(daily return, RV innovation), no look-ahead): traditional-approach OOS R-squared in 'high' months 6.8%-16.4% (1-month classification) and 6.5%-20.7% (3-month); 'low' months -13.5% to 0.0% (claim's -12.4% was the 1-month-classification minimum only); high-minus-low 14.4-29.8 pp; author reports no Wald statistics for these subsamples and calls them 'somewhat informal'. In-sample 1990-2016 Table 7: high months R-squared 11.7%-17.9% (t 3.8-4.9), betas 8.7-11.4; low months 0.4%-2.7% (t 0.7-0.8), betas 0.03-1.6. The variance-beta method's edge is regime-concentrated (OOS R-squared 2.0%-5.9% in low-correlation months vs 7.9%-16.1% in high). IDEAS/Semantic Scholar citing lists show no replication or critique; Dew-Becker & Giglio (Chicago Fed WP 2025-17) find index-option alphas (the VRP itself) indistinguishable from zero over the past ~15 years, which bears on the premium but not on this predictor (claims 4, 5 merged).

Sources: https://sjpyun.github.io/VRPTS.pdf

### The ex-ante VIX futures premium (1-month futures price minus an ARMA(2,2) VIX forecast estimated only on pre-2004 data) is a near-unbiased predictor of realized returns to a rolling 1-month VIX-futures strategy (coefficient about 1, concentrated in the first month), and it counter-intuitively FALLS, sometimes turning negative, when realized vol, VIX, VVIX or skew rise, because dealers and asset managers cut long VIX-futures hedges; the premium began falling in early September 2008 ahead of the worst of the crisis and was deeply negative in late Feb-early Mar 2020.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 (both claims)

Cheng, The VIX Premium (RFS 2019, 32(1):180-227), Mar 2004-Nov 2015. Table 4: monthly coefficient 0.92 (NW s.e. 0.29, R-squared 8.8%, T 140); 2010-onward 1.20 (0.41); weekly 0.83 (0.22); daily 1.07 (0.23); six alternative HAR/realized-variance forecast models all within one s.e. of 1; t+5 monthly 0.07 (0.30). Table 2: a 1-SD rise in monthly S&P 500 RV (6.4 pp) lowers the premium 0.45 points (0.43 SD; 1 SD of premium = 1.04 points); 1-SD VVIX (14.0 points) -0.36 points (0.35 SD); SPX implied skew -0.46 SD, VIX -0.35 SD, CBOE SKEW -0.24 SD; partial reversal over 2-3 months (not for SKEW); all forecast models give negative contemporaneous coefficients. Verifier corrections: the 2010-2015 subsample range is -0.13 to -0.28 SD (not -0.18 to -0.28), all negative but only 3 of 5 (VVIX, SPX skew, SKEW) significant at 5%; RV and VIX are not. CFTC TFF positions: 1-SD weekly RV shock (5.3%) lowers dealer net positions 0.25 SD and raises hedge-fund positions 0.17 SD; dealers/asset managers net long since 2010. Independent corroboration cited by verifiers: Cheng (RAPS 2020, through Mar 2020) monthly beta 1.32 (0.43) full sample, 0.92 (0.30) excluding 2020; Hu & Jacobs (JFQA, 2004-2022, recursive OOS) slopes 0.44-0.52 with Hodrick t 2.7-2.9; Nielsen & Posselt (IRFA 2024, 2012-2019) reproduce the negative premium-risk relation and attribute it to VIX-ETP outflows (1-SD flow shock = +0.33 SD premium change); Aragon-Mehra-Wahal (2018/2020) cite it approvingly. Caveat: the forecast model is out-of-sample but the predictive regressions are full-sample; the result concerns VIX futures, not equity returns (claims 0, 2 merged).

Sources: https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/682f3c99-5d7d-4dee-b4b4-dafa83785523/download

### For realized-volatility expansion (question 1c), the one verified signal is the CHANGE in the VIX futures premium: a one-standard-deviation monthly FALL in the premium predicts roughly a 3-percentage-point rise in next-month S&P 500 realized volatility (against 15% unconditional) and a 16-point rise in the realized volatility of the VIX-futures strategy (against 61%), i.e., cheapening volatility insurance precedes higher, not lower, realized risk.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0

Cheng (RFS 2019) Table 5, monthly OLS of the change in ex-post volatility on three lags of the change in the premium and of volatility, NW s.e. with 3 lags, Mar 2004-Nov 2015: market RV (5-minute SPX incl. overnight) coefficient on lagged premium change -2.77 (1.07), T 137; strategy RV -15.1 (4.6), T 136; 2010-2015 subsample -2.20 (1.15, t about 1.9, not significant at 5% by the paper's convention) and -29.3 (10.2). With a 1-SD premium change of 1.04 points, -2.769 x 1.04 = -2.9 (about 3 points) and -15.141 x 1.04 = -15.7 (about 16 points); unconditional RV mean 14.64%. Author's framing: 'If anything, falling premiums predict higher risk.' In-sample monthly regression; the premium itself uses a pre-2004 forecast model. Consistent with the Feb-Mar 2020 episode (Cheng 2020: premium deeply negative before the VIX record highs; Carvalho 2021 MSc thesis re-finds the puzzle through Dec 2020), but no peer-reviewed re-estimation of this specific regression on post-2015 data was located (claim 1).

Sources: https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/682f3c99-5d7d-4dee-b4b4-dafa83785523/download

### The VIX term-structure slope does not forecast subsequent changes in the VIX at 1-month or 1-quarter horizons beyond the mean reversion already captured by the current VIX level (expectations hypothesis rejected): an inverted or backwardated VIX curve is a signal about time-varying variance risk premia, not a forecast that volatility will keep rising. The rejection is disputed at 6-12 month horizons but not at the short horizons relevant to regime signals.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0

Johnson, Risk Premia and the VIX Term Structure (JFQA 2017, 52(6):2461-2490), OptionMetrics SPX options 1996-2013, 4,445 daily obs, VIX-squared at 1,2,3,6,9,12 months, Newey-West s.e. with 1.5x overlap lags. Table 3: 10 EH tests (5 'decay', 5 'change'; k = 1 month for m = 1,2 and k = 3 months for m = 3,6,9); every b-hat < 1, EH (b = 1) rejected with p-values 0.0%-4.6%. Adding the current VIX-squared as control (loadings -0.25 to -0.51, s.e. 0.047-0.080, all highly significant) shrinks the change-regression b-hats from +0.23 to +0.65 (three about 3 s.e. from zero) to -0.25 to +0.06, all insignificant: 'it drowns out any predictability afforded by the expectations hypothesis term'. Verifier correction: in the uncontrolled decay regressions 3 of 5 b-hats are negative (two positive), all 5 insignificant with R-squared 0.0%-2.3%; with the VIX-squared control all 5 are negative and significant (equivalent to b about 0 in the change regressions). Qualifying literature found by verifier: Hollstein, Prokopczuk & Wese Simen (J. Futures Markets 2019; monthly 1996-2015, 236 obs) cannot reject pure EH at 6-12 month horizons (b = 1.04 for 12m vs twelve 1m contracts) and attribute Johnson's rejection to daily overlapping observations, but themselves reject pure EH at the 2-month forward horizon (5%), so both agree EH fails or is weakest at <= 1 quarter. Asensio (Quantitative Finance 2020, VIX futures to about 2018) finds the slope predicts direction but not magnitude of short-end VIX moves and attributes violations to ETN long-VIX demand; Nossman & Vilhelmsson (JAI 2009) reject EH without, but not with, a stochastic-volatility risk-premium adjustment; Koeter (2024 WP, 1996-2019) finds the slope PC negatively predicts next-month realized variance alongside level. Data end 2013 (claim 6).

Sources: https://www.travislakejohnson.com/pdfs/Johnson%20VIXTS%202017%20(JFQA).pdf

### The second principal component of the VIX-squared term structure (SLOPE) negatively predicts next-day and next-month returns on volatility assets (synthetic variance swaps, constant-maturity VIX futures, ATM straddles), and when the curve is inverted (lowest SLOPE quintile) the variance risk premium changes sign so that long-volatility positions are paid; a purely walk-forward rule (buy 30-day straddles when SLOPE is in its bottom historical quintile, otherwise sell them) beat unconditional straddle selling by a factor of 4.8 (11.9%/yr) over 2000-2013, though a substantial part of the gain came from being long straddles in late 2008 and returns use bid-ask midpoints.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 (both claims)

Johnson (JFQA 2017) Tables 4-7, Fig. 2, 1996-2013 (VIX futures 2004-2013): 36 specifications (3 assets x 6 maturities x 2 horizons), SLOPE negative in all 36, 33 significant; high-minus-low SLOPE quintile spread 29-181 bps/day and 7.4%-36.4%/month (Table 6 text gives -8.25% to -36.67%, an internal inconsistency); in the lowest SLOPE quintile 17 of 18 variance assets earn positive returns (10 significant), e.g. 12-month straddles +30 bps/day over the risk-free rate; incremental to implied-minus-expected variance (which itself negatively predicts 11 of 18 assets); coefficients stay negative and significant for all but 2 assets after controlling for SPX implied skew, Treasury NOISE illiquidity, broker-dealer leverage, a 21-day crash indicator and lagged returns. OOS: SLOPE-only regressions have positive OOS R-squared in 33 of 36 cases (next-month straddles 2.3%-10.4%, next-month VIX futures 3.3%-8.8%) and beat the unrestricted 6-PC model in 33 of 36, but these use a leave-out window design the author says does not represent real-time value. Trading rule: 1996-1999 training, expanding-window PCA each day, Reg T 20% margin, 3,437 days 2000-2013; quintile-spread results survive removing 2008-2009 and the bottom 5% of SLOPE days at reduced magnitude; midpoint returns overstate achievable returns. Full-sample PCs introduce mild look-ahead (acknowledged). Relevance to the research question: this is the clearest verified evidence that a backwardated VIX curve marks a state in which realized variance tends to exceed what the curve prices (a two-tailed, long-vol state), rather than a directional equity signal. No post-2017 contradiction search could be run (search budget exhausted) (claims 7, 8 merged).

Sources: https://www.travislakejohnson.com/pdfs/Johnson%20VIXTS%202017%20(JFQA).pdf

### SLOPE does not predict S&P 500 equity returns (an untabulated result), despite being positively correlated with implied-minus-statistical variance; the equity-return predictability of the VIX term structure reported by Bakshi, Panayotov & Skoulakis (2011) and Feunou et al. (2014) is attributed to other principal components, none of which predicts variance-asset returns. Term-structure shape therefore forecasts volatility-asset returns while VRP-type spreads (Bollerslev-Tauchen-Zhou 2009, Drechsler-Yaron 2011) forecast equity returns.

Confidence: low; vote 3-0

Verbatim statement on p. 2465 of Johnson (JFQA 2017): 'In untabulated tests, I find that SLOPE does not predict equity returns despite being positively correlated with implied minus statistical variance. Instead, the equity return predictability afforded by the VIX term structure in Bakshi, Panayotov, and Skoulakis (2011) and Feunou et al. (2014) is attributable to other PCs of the VIX term structure, none of which predicts variance asset returns.' No coefficients, t-statistics, horizon or OOS metric are reported, so the null cannot be independently checked; sample 1996-2013 with in-sample PCs. Johnson is agnostic on mechanism (mispricing or demand-based price impact per Garleanu-Pedersen-Poteshman 2009, or a variance risk premium outside the BTZ/DY models); the 'separation' framing is a mild interpretive tightening. Confidence is low because the finding is single-source and untabulated, although the vote was unanimous that the paper states it (claim 9).

Sources: https://www.travislakejohnson.com/pdfs/Johnson%20VIXTS%202017%20(JFQA).pdf

### VVIX negatively predicts returns on tail hedges (OTM S&P 500 puts and OTM VIX calls) one day to 3-4 weeks ahead and dominates the VIX level and four jump/tail indices in that role; this is evidence that VVIX prices tail-risk insurance, not evidence that VVIX forecasts equity drawdowns or realized volatility, and it is in-sample over a short, crisis-dominated May 2006-Jan 2012 window with no out-of-sample test.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 (all three claims)

Park, Volatility of Volatility and Tail Risk Premiums (FEDS 2013-54, Aug 1 2013; published as Volatility-of-volatility and tail risk hedging returns, J. Financial Markets 26, 2015, 38-63; numbers taken from the Fed-hosted PDF because SSRN returned 403). Table 4, one-day-ahead predictive regressions, Newey-West t-stats, delta-hedged residual option returns: a 1-SD rise in VVIX lowers next-day OTM SPX put returns 1.32%-2.19% (t -4.48 to -5.54; deep -2.03, medium -2.19, slight -1.32) and next-day OTM VIX call returns 0.68%-1.01% (t -3.55 to -4.44), larger for deeper strikes; adjusted R-squared 2.0%-4.7%. Table 5: predictability persists 3 weeks ahead for SPX puts (all moneyness, 95-99%) and 4 weeks for medium/deep OTM VIX calls, attributed to VVIX persistence (daily AR(1) 0.924, weekly autocorrelation 0.759, half-life 2.51 weeks). Controls (Regressions 2-6): VIX level (t -0.34 to 0.86), Barndorff-Nielsen-Shephard jump variation (t -0.53 to 0.60), Du-Kapadia JTIX (t -0.71 to 0.37), Bollerslev-Todorov fear index (t 0.17 to 0.62), Kelly TAIL (t -1.51 to 0.18) all insignificant with or without VVIX (footnote 10), while VVIX stays at 99% (t -2.84 to -5.54) except medium OTM VIX calls in the truncated FI sample (t -1.59, footnote 9). ### The CBOE-methodology SKEW index is a weak, fragile and often-misread drawdown signal: it only marginally predicts monthly S&P 500 drops of 5% or more, loses significance once tail-risk or Goyal-Welch macro-financial controls are added, its daily change adds no significant power for next-day index returns, and it is negatively correlated with VIX (-23%), contradicting the 'high SKEW = high fear' reading.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 (both claims)

Bevilacqua & Tunaru, The SKEW Index: Extracting What Has Been Left (J. Financial Stability 53, 2021), OptionMetrics SPX options Jan 1996-Dec 2017, monthly end-of-month, authors' Bakshi-Kapadia-Madan construction. Table 6 logit of a 5%-drop dummy on lagged SKEW controlling for the lagged dummy: -0.062* (h=1), -0.095* (h=3), -0.093** (h=6), -0.060* (h=12), pseudo-R-squared 7.8%, 8.2%, 4.6%, 2.2%; with TAIL/Hellinger/CATFIN/CRASH controls -0.055 n.s. at 1 month (10%-level at 3 and 6 months); loses all power once four Goyal-Welch principal components are added. Table 10 pseudo-OOS (expanding window, 1996-2005 train, 2006-2017 test, Campbell-Thompson R2OS vs AR benchmark, Clark-West): total SKEW 1.30%*, 1.21%*, 0.62%*, 2.27%** at 1/3/6/12 months. Table 1: VIX-SKEW correlation -0.23***. Liu & Faff, Hitting SKEW for SIX (Economic Modelling 64, 2017, 449-464), SPX options 1996-2013: 'the daily change of SIX (SKEW) adds (does not add) significant explanatory power for predicting the one-day ahead return'; abstract calls SKEW's usefulness questionable; full text paywalled so the protocol is presumed in-sample, and the horse race is against the authors' own index (verifier: 'drawdown' slightly overstates what was tested, which is one-day-ahead returns).
