# Deep research: non-Gaussian regime and crash-onset methods with out-of-sample evidence

**Question.** Which non-Gaussian methods have documented OUT-OF-SAMPLE evidence for detecting the onset of volatility regimes or predicting equity-index drawdowns and crash risk at horizons of days to a few months? Cover: (1) multifractal analysis (MF-DFA, generalized Hurst exponents h(q), multifractal spectrum width, Hurst/R-S, DFA on absolute returns) as regime or crash-risk indicators; (2) heavy-tailed, extreme-value and quantile/rank-based or distribution-free approaches (CoVaR, tail-index monitoring, copula tail dependence, rank correlations); (3) regime-switching and hidden-state models with non-Gaussian innovations, and machine-learning classifiers with shape constraints; (4) inputs from the vol complex: VIX, VVIX, VIX term structure (VIX/VIX3M, VIX9D), MOVE, SKEW, realized vs implied vol, cross-sectional dispersion and average correlation, volume and the volatility of volume; (5) which published or practitioner results later proved to be in-sample artifacts or failed out of sample. Prefer academic sources (econophysics, financial econometrics, Journal of Financial Economics, Quantitative Finance, Physica A, SSRN) and practitioner research 2000-2026 with specific performance metrics (AUC, hit rates, Sharpe or drawdown deltas, hazard ratios) and the validation protocol used (walk-forward, purged cross-validation, permutation tests). Equity indices (S&P 500) primarily; daily data; US market.

**Summary.** Among the methods for which claims survived adversarial verification, only two families have genuine out-of-sample (OOS) evidence at days-to-months horizons, and neither is a clean crash-onset detector: (i) a marked Hawkes/ETAS self-exciting point process calibrated on S&P 500 daily returns 1957-2008 and run with fixed parameters over Sep 2008-Jan 2013 flags a 5%-tail daily loss within 5 days with hit rate 0.60-0.61, false-alarm rate 0.18-0.19 and Hanssen-Kuiper skill 0.41-0.43, but Student-t GARCH/GJR early-warning systems match it for crashes (KSS 0.42) and ETAS wins only at the 99% quantile and for two-sided extremes (KSS 0.64 vs 0.28); (ii) cross-sectional Hill tail-index estimators predict OOS but with the 'wrong' sign and target for drawdown warning: Kelly-Jiang's monthly index forecasts HIGHER future excess returns (OOS R2 0.3% and insignificant at 1 month, 4.5-20.1% and significant at 1-5 years; sign flips with pre-1963 data per Goyal-Welch-Zafirov 2024) and was flat through 2007-09, while Almeida et al.'s daily intraday version forecasts LOWER future VIX^2/realized variance at 1-22 days (OOS R2 1-6%, no OOS significance tests, unpublished). The multifractal/local-Hurst literature (Grech-Mazur 2004, Kristoufek 2010, da Fonseca et al. 2013, Morales et al. 2012) has no OOS evidence at all: every result is a retrospective visual read of 3-5 ex-post-selected crashes or firms with parameters tuned on the same data and no false-alarm counts, and the one independent pre-specified test (Coen, Piovani & Torluccio 2012, 35 series) found 3/35 hits plus many false alarms, making the DFA 'Hurst-drop' crash rule the clearest documented in-sample artifact. Alpha-stable GARCH crash probabilities moved only ~0.2 percentage points in the month before Lehman, and CATFIN's early-warning threshold remains partly full-sample with monthly macro rather than equity-drawdown targets. No claims about vol-complex inputs (VIX term structure, VVIX, SKEW, MOVE, dispersion/correlation, volume), regime-switching or hidden-state models with non-Gaussian innovations, shape-constrained ML classifiers, CoVaR, or copula tail dependence survived verification, so those parts of the question are unanswered by this evidence set.

## Findings

### The rolling-DFA local Hurst/alpha 'crash signature' (Grech & Mazur 2004 on DJIA; the Grech-Pamula/Czarnecki 2008 four-condition rule - H in decreasing trend, H21 <~ 0.5, H5 <~ 0.45, local minimum < 0.4 - as applied by Kristoufek 2010 to the Prague PX index) has NO out-of-sample evidence. Every supporting study is a retrospective visual inspection of 3-4 ex-post-selected turning points with the window length N=215 calibrated on the same series, no enumeration of all signals fired, no false-alarm rate, hit rate, AUC or formal hypothesis test; the authors themselves restrict claimed success to 'quiet' trending markets and concede predictions become 'troublesome' in 1995-2003; and Kristoufek's own single shuffled-returns surrogate reproduced the full pre-crash pattern at artificial points, rescued only by a non-formalised 'stable trend' precondition.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 on each of claims [0], [1], [6], [7] (merged)

Grech & Mazur (Physica A 336, 2004; arXiv cond-mat/0311627): N=215 sessions (~10 trading months) chosen as 'the first local minimum below N=240' of the DFA estimator's standard deviation computed on 500 DJIA subseries per N, with the authors conceding 'this minimum is not strong'; ~9% statistical uncertainty in alpha; results insensitive for 190<=N<=230; box sizes 5<tau<N/5; alpha uses past data only (real-time computable) but is evaluated only on 1929, 1987, 1998 plus a 1995-2003 commentary. Validation is Figs. 4-6 ('A very clear decreasing trend in local alpha is visible'; 'To see if it was a chance, we checked two other major crashes'); the only quantitative argument is an informal error-propagation comparison for 1929 (~18% uncertainty vs ~33% change), not a hypothesis test. Conclusions: 'The best results... seem to be obtained if quiet situation exists... This situation took place in 1929, 1987 and 1998. Otherwise, the alpha-exponent contains artifacts... predictions are troublesome'; for 1995-2003 'alpha-exponent loses its sensitivity'. Kristoufek (Acta Phys. Pol. B 41(6), 2010), PX daily 7/1/1997-12/31/2009, T=215, v_min=5, v_max=T/5: 3 of 4 ex-post-chosen tops (Mar 2000, Mar 2005, Oct/Nov 2007; miss Feb 2006) show the pattern with a stated lead of 'several upcoming trading days'; no false-positive count, no holdout. Sec. 4 surrogate: ONE shuffled-returns PX series with identical return distribution 'has several points which could be detected as the turning points' (e.g. Oct 2000, Feb 2006), attributed to DFA-induced autocorrelation in differences of rolling H at lags that are multiples of v_min; dismissed because the shuffled series lacks 'clear trends and stable behavior', a criterion never given a numeric form. Provenance nuance: Kristoufek attributes the rule to Grech & Pamula / Czarnecki et al. (2008), whose preprint (arXiv:0708.0353) states H21<=0.5 and H5<~0.45 (thresholds transposed relative to Kristoufek) and whose own real-time July 2007 WIG assessment said the crash pattern 'has not been formed so far' immediately before the WIG peak. Term counts across the primary texts for AUC/hit rate/out-of-sample/permutation/walk-forward: zero. Four merged claims [0],[1],[6],[7], all 3-0, all verified against full primary texts.

Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0311627, https://www.actaphys.uj.edu.pl/R/41/6/1223/pdf, https://arxiv.org/abs/0708.0353

### The single independent, pre-specified test of the Hurst-drop crash rule (Coen, Piovani & Torluccio 2012; 14 indices incl. NASDAQ + 21 stocks, daily, DFA local H on 500-day windows) falsifies it: the rule flagged the 2007-09 crash within three months before each series' price peak in only 3 of 35 series while generating many detections not followed by any fall (e.g. 15 CAC 40 detection dates over 2004-2012), and pre-peak H statistics (min, H21, H5 over the 21 days before the peak) were not systematically lower than at arbitrary dates. The authors reject the hypothesis that H falls materially before a crisis, which makes the published Grech/Kristoufek successes the clearest documented case in this evidence set of an in-sample artifact.

Confidence: medium; vote [4] 3-0; [5] 2-1

Corporate Ownership & Control 10(1), 291-308, DOI 10.22495/cocv10i1c2art7 (University of Bologna authors). Sec. 3.1-3.2: the Czarnecki (2008) procedure implemented as a 21-day negative regression slope in H, then H21<0.5 and H5<0.45, then a value of H<0.4 in the 21-day period, plus the authors' own 30-day refractory condition. p.297: 'Out of 35 indices/stocks only in 3 cases was the coming crisis forecast... this procedure identified many dates that were not followed by any fall... this hypothesis has to be rejected. In short, Hurst exponent analysis does not seem able to forecast crises.' Appendix B CAC 40: exactly 15 detection dates 2004-04-22 to 2012-07-13, none in the three months before the 2007-06-01 peak. Table 2 ('Number of data superior than Before max date', Min/H21/H5): 12 May 2006 = 18/19/20 (weak support), 15 Mar 2012 = 9/8/9, 20 Nov 2008 = 5/4/5; Table 3: 21 days after peak = 14/18/16, 27 Nov 2004 = 11/11/12, 1 Sep 2009 = 12/11/13 - i.e. pre-peak H values exceed arbitrary-date values roughly 25-62% of the time, no systematic pre-peak drop. Qualifications: (a) 500-day DFA window vs the 215-session window the rule was calibrated on, so not an exact replication; (b) 20 Nov 2008 is not arbitrary - it was chosen as the date minimising mean H21 across series, so its 4-5 count is by construction; (c) denominators inconsistent (32-34 comparable series, only 21 at 27 Nov 2004); (d) the 3 'hits' are loosely counted (ENEL and Volkswagen's Oct-2008 squeeze are strict hits; the NASDAQ detection at 119 days falls just outside three calendar months); (e) purely descriptive counts, no permutation test. Low-impact venue (2015 CiteScore 0.2), 5 citations, no rebuttal found. Claim [4] 3-0; claim [5] 2-1 (the dissent concerned the mislabelled 20 Nov 2008 date and denominators, not the direction of the result).

Sources: https://virtusinterpress.org/ON-THE-UTILITY-OF-THE-HURST.html, https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/9661/8ff4d32869c6fd838932dd1f48a7a6fdbb12.pdf

### The multifractal 'area variation rate' (AVR) crash indicator of da Fonseca, Ferreira, Muruganandam & Cerdeira (Physica A 392, 2013) is an in-sample, ex-post construction: on DJIA daily closes (N=1000, T=1, l=1) the authors assert the AVR rises >=10x above a matched white-noise level at least one trading week before the 1929, 1987 and 2008 crash dates (almost two weeks for 1929/1987), but the evidence is three pre-selected events, parameters N, T, l are 'chosen as the best set... depend[ing] on the stability of results' against historically recognised crises, the 2008 pre-Lehman rise is absent in the paper's own anchor figure (>=10x jumps occur 10 and 20 trading days AFTER 15 Sep 2008), the 'analogous specific heat' second lobe is coincident rather than leading (it requires the crash-day observation inside the window and vanishes when it is removed), and no hit rate, false-alarm rate, lead-time distribution, AUC, walk-forward or permutation test is reported.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 on each of claims [2], [3] (merged)

Verified against arXiv:1204.3136v3 and the published Physica A author copy (identical text). Lead-time sentence: 'the AVR rises above noise at least one week before the crash for 1929, 1987 and 2008'; noise benchmark = Gaussian white noise with same mean/variance, 'rises above noise' = >=1 order of magnitude above ~10^-3 fluctuations. Authors' own 2008 caveats: 'Although there is a jump in the curve, it is well below the other curves'; 'no well defined second maximum in ASpH for 2008'; the Lehman date 'may have produced the successive jumps at n_s=60 and n_s=70'. Verifier figure inspection (300 dpi): Fig. 2 cyan 2008 curve flat until n_s=50 (Lehman day), step to ~0.012 at n_s=50, jumps to ~0.085 at n_s=60 (29 Sep 2008, -7.0%) and ~0.10 at n_s=70 (13 Oct 2008, +11.1%); Figs. 3(c)/4(b) plot the same series shifted ~10-17 index units earlier, so the paper's figures disagree on 2008 timing by more than the claimed lead; for 1929/1987 the 'lead' steps coincide with large-|dx| days already inside the window (AVR behaves as a record-size-move detector). Second lobe inherited from Canessa (2001): 'these curves present two lobes when the time series of N elements contains the data point of the crash... the maxima... diminishes when the single data point from the day of the crash is removed and it disappears when the data of the neighboring days is deleted'. Hedge: 'the predictive ability of the algorithm seems to depend on the values of T'. Only false-alarm remark is qualitative ('sometimes the AVR presents unexpected jumps... most of those jumps do not exhibit the pattern observed in the famous crashes'). Term counts in full text: AUC 0, hit rate 0, out-of-sample 0, permutation 0, bootstrap 0, p-value 0. Semantic Scholar: 8 citations in 13 years, no replication or OOS test; Jiang-Xie-Zhou-Sornette review (Rep. Prog. Phys. 2019) says only that AVR 'has potential power in identifying historical crashes'. A related claim that the 2011 US-downgrade sell-off was a documented miss was REFUTED (1-2) because the authors frame it as correctly distinguishing a 'scare' from a systemic crisis. Claims [2],[3] both 3-0.

Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.3136, https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04750

### The exponentially weighted generalized Hurst exponent H^w(q=1) of Morales, Di Matteo, Gramatica & Aste (Physica A 391, 2012) - daily log-returns of NYSE stocks 1996-2009, 750-day windows, 250-day weighting time, 50-day shifts - shows a rising trend crossing 0.5 before/into the 2007-10 crisis only for five ex-post-selected bailed-out firms (AIG, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Washington Mutual, GM); it is not a uniform warning even within financials (Lehman stays ~0.5 until an abrupt end-of-sample jump; American Express and Morgan Stanley fluctuate about 0.5; Basic Materials firms trend down), the multifractality measure H^w(1)-H^w(1.5) rises only once the unstable period is reached (coincident, attributed to large price fluctuations rather than a change in the stochastic process), and there is no index-level, hit-rate, false-positive or out-of-sample evaluation.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 on each of claims [8], [9], [10] (merged)

Verified against the full arXiv text. Data: 395 NYSE stocks, 1 Jan 1996-30 Apr 2009, log-returns; parameters Delta t=750, theta=250, 50-day shift (Figs. 4-7, 10, 11 captions). Abstract: 'Financial firms bailed-out... show a neat increase with time of the generalized Hurst exponent in the period preceding the unfolding of the crisis'; body text locates the 0.5 crossing 'towards the end of the time-period when the crisis fully unfolded'. Non-generality verbatim: 'even in the Financial sector itself, the increasing trend found for the bailed-out companies is not common to others; for instance... American Express Co and Morgan Stanley show stable behaviors, with wGHE values steadily fluctuating about 0.5'; 'LBH keeps steady around 0.5, except for a decrease towards the end of the period followed by a sudden leap upwards'; Basic Materials (Noble Energy, Occidental) 'very definite overall decreasing trend'. Multifractality: 'this difference remains stable for most of the time... it increases as soon as the unstable period is reached'; 'the behavior observed in the empirical data is not necessarily related to a change of the stochastic process underlying the financial time series'; 'increase in the multifractality... is likely to occur in the presence of large price fluctuations'. Grep for false/hit rate/out-of-sample/alarm/forecast/predict: nothing relevant; only 'might suggest a tool to assess risk'. Selection: 'we have focused our attention on the companies most severely involved in the unfolding of the 2007-2010 credit crunch'. Note the sign is OPPOSITE to the Grech/Kristoufek 'Hurst drop' literature (rising vs falling H before crisis), underscoring the absence of a stable, pre-specified multifractal regime signature. Three merged claims [8],[9],[10], all 3-0; single primary paper, so confidence capped at medium.

Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.0465

### The only method in this evidence set with a genuine fixed-parameter out-of-sample crash-alarm test and classification metrics is the marked Hawkes/ETAS self-exciting point-process early warning system of Gresnigt, Kole & Franses (Tinbergen DP 2014-067; J. Banking & Finance 2015): calibrated once on S&P 500 daily returns 2 Jan 1957-1 Sep 2008 (687 events in 13,738 days at the 95% quantile) and run with fixed parameters over 1 Sep 2008-1 Jan 2013, an alarm when P(5%-tail negative return within 5 trading days) > 0.5 achieves hit rate 0.60-0.61, false-alarm rate 0.18-0.19 and KSS 0.41-0.43 (crisis sub-window Sep 2008-Jan 2010: hit 0.84-0.87, FAR 0.38-0.41, KSS 0.45-0.46; absolute extremes 2008-13: hit 0.72-0.74, FAR 0.16-0.18, KSS 0.55-0.56). However, Student-t GARCH(1,1)/GJR(1,1) EWS match or slightly beat ETAS for crashes at the 95-98% quantiles (KSS 0.42/0.42 vs 0.42/0.41 at 95%); ETAS wins only at the 99% crash quantile (0.40/0.39 vs 0.27/0.37) and for two-sided extremes (0.64/0.64 vs 0.28/0.28 at 99%), so the OOS skill documents volatility/event clustering rather than a non-Gaussian edge over GARCH for drawdown prediction.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 on each of claims [11], [12] (merged)

Verified against the primary PDF text. Sec. 3: 'data of the S&P 500 index over a period from 2 January, 1957, to 1 September, 2008 to calibrate our models and 5 years thereafter for an out-of-sample evaluation... 687 events over a time period of 13,738 trading days'. Sec. 5: EWS uses the conditional intensity from the estimated ETAS parameters plus the realised event history; threshold 0.5; event = occurrence within 5 days. Table 9 (models A,C,E,G), crashes 2008-13: hit rate 0.61/0.61/0.60/0.60, FAR 0.19/0.19/0.19/0.18, KSS 0.42/0.43/0.41/0.41; 2008-10: 0.87/0.87/0.85/0.84, 0.41/0.40/0.39/0.38, 0.45/0.46/0.46/0.46; extremes 2008-13: 0.74/0.74/0.72/0.72, 0.18/0.18/0.17/0.16, 0.56/0.56/0.55/0.56. 'The KSS of all EWS are positive... whereas KSS of the Poisson-EWS is zero.' Table 13 (crashes, OOS Sep 2008-Jan 2013): 95% GARCH 0.42, GJR 0.42, ETAS-Pow 0.42, ETAS-Exp 0.41; 96-98% GARCH/GJR 0.43/0.43, 0.46/0.46, 0.40/0.42 vs ETAS 0.41/0.39, 0.45/0.44, 0.36/0.37; 99% ETAS 0.40/0.39 vs GARCH 0.27, GJR 0.37. Table 14 (absolute extremes): ETAS leads at every quantile (0.56/0.55 vs 0.43/0.45 at 95%; 0.64/0.64 vs 0.28/0.28 at 99%). Sec. 6.2: 'Compared to the ETAS models the KSS of the EWS for crashes are slightly higher. However the EWS based on the ETAS model outperform the EWS based on the GARCH-type models for extremes, especially at higher quantiles.' Sec. 6.1 (in-sample): ETAS fitted to GARCH standardized residuals retains significant self-excitation; 'only the alpha parameter is not significant at a 5% level'. Limitations: a single OOS window dominated by the 2008-09 crisis (high 5-day base rate inflates raw hit rates); event threshold is the in-sample 95% quantile; 'fixed parameters' is inferred from the design, not stated as 'no re-estimation'; no significance test of KSS differences; only trivial homogeneous-Poisson benchmark besides GARCH; the ACD-type alternatives failed. Two merged claims [11],[12], both 3-0.

Sources: https://papers.tinbergen.nl/14067.pdf

### The Kelly-Jiang cross-sectional tail index (monthly Hill power-law estimator pooling all daily CRSP stock returns, share codes 10-11, Jan 1963-Dec 2010, threshold at the 5th percentile of the pooled cross-section so it expands/contracts with volatility; monthly AR(1) 0.927 despite non-overlapping estimation) has documented recursive out-of-sample return predictability, but with a POSITIVE sign and at long horizons: a one-SD rise forecasts +4.5%/+4.0%/+3.7%/+3.2% annualised CRSP value-weighted excess returns at 1m/1y/3y/5y (Hodrick t 2.1/2.0/2.4/2.7; in-sample R2 0.7/6.1/16.6/20.9%), and expanding-window OOS R2 (first forecast month 120, historical-mean benchmark) is 0.3% at 1 month - NOT significant under Clark-McCracken ENC-NEW - versus 4.5%/15.7%/20.1% (significant at 5%) at 1/3/5 years, while nearly all Goyal-Welch predictors and realized volatility have negative OOS R2. It is therefore a discount-rate/risk-premium predictor, not a drawdown warning, and the short-horizon OOS power relevant to the question is economically negligible; Goyal-Welch-Zafirov (RFS 2024) replicate it on the 1963+ sample but find the sign flips and significance vanishes when extended back to 1926, with poor investment performance.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 on each of claims [13], [14], [15] (merged)

NBER WP 19375 (published RFS 27, 2014) verified verbatim: 'we estimate the tail exponent month-by-month by applying Hill's (1975) power law estimator to the set of daily return observations for all stocks in month t'; 'we fix the threshold at the 5th percentile of the cross section distribution period-by-period... the threshold varies as the cross section distribution fans out and compresses over time, which mitigates undue effects of volatility'; 'The tail series is highly persistent, possessing a monthly AR(1) coefficient of 0.927' (footnote 23: likely downward biased by estimation noise). Table 3 row Tail: 4.54/2.08/0.7 (1m), 4.02/2.04/6.1 (1y), 3.65/2.40/16.6 (3y), 3.16/2.65/20.9 (5y); Table 4: 1-month tail coefficient 4.04-4.86 with t 1.83-2.15 across all 13 Goyal-Welch controls. Table 5 OOS R2 (%): Tail 0.3 / 4.5* / 15.7* / 20.1*, stars on 1y/3y/5y only; stock volatility -2.5/-25.0/-28.1/-22.1; long-term yield -0.9/-4.6/20.6*/10.2*; all 13 alternatives negative at 1 month. Protocol text: 'beginning at t = 120... The estimation window is then extended by one month... repeated until the full sample has been exhausted', benchmark = historical average return (recursive expanding window, though Table 5 notes loosely say 'rolling'). Summary: 'tail risk is positively and significantly related to market discount rates'. Replication: Goyal, Welch & Zafirov (RFS 37(11), 2024) near-replication t=2.32, extension to 2021 t=2.16; homologous post-1963 IS R2 0.75%, OOS R2 0.68% both significant; but with 1926 backward extension IS t=0.26, OOS R2 negative, sign flips in the 1926- first-half subsample (-0.31 vs 1.81), poor investment performance; they 'would not outright dismiss tail risk'. Known concern: long-horizon starred results use overlapping observations where ENC-NEW size distortions and OOS R2 inflation are well documented (Boudoukh-Richardson-Whitelaw 2008). Three merged claims [13],[14],[15], all 3-0; primary RFS paper plus independent 2024 replication.

Sources: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19375/w19375.pdf, https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/37/11/3490/7735363

### As a crash-onset or volatility-regime detector the Kelly-Jiang cross-sectional Hill tail index fails by the authors' own description of their series: it rose only in the months AFTER the October 1987 crash, reached its highest post-2000 level at the early-2003 market trough (with the value-weighted index already down 49% and NASDAQ 78%), and was 'roughly flat through the 2007-2009 financial crisis and recession'. The authors attribute the 2008 non-response (citing Brownlees-Engle-Kelly 2011) to the crisis being a predictable-volatility episode rather than a fat-tail episode, and note the 5th-percentile threshold - which has a 60% correlation with market volatility - absorbs volatility spikes by design.

Confidence: high; vote 3-0 (claim [16])

Verbatim from pp. 16-17 of NBER w19375: 'Tail risk recedes in the four bull market years leading up to 1987, rising quickly in the months following the October crash. During the technology boom, tail risk retreats sharply but briefly, rising to its highest post-2000 level amid the early 2003 market trough. At this time the value-weighted index was down 49% from its 2000 high and NASDAQ was 78% off its peak. During the last half of the decade, tail risk hovers close to its mean, and is roughly flat through the 2007-2009 financial crisis and recession.' 2008 explanation (hedged as 'potentially consistent with the account... in Brownlees, Engle and Kelly (2011)'): 'the financial crisis was characterized by soaring volatility, but that this volatility was predictable over short horizons using standard volatility forecasting models and that volatility-adjusted residuals do not appear extreme compared to their historical distribution.' Design: 'The lower tail threshold has a 60% correlation with market volatility'; 'If volatility rises dramatically but the shape of return tails is unchanged, then a widening of the threshold will absorb the effect of volatility changes and leave estimates of the tail exponent unaffected.' Qualification: 'fails' is the synthesis's inference - Kelly & Jiang never propose the measure as a crash-onset detector; it is positioned as a priced risk factor. The finding is the authors' own description of a fixed 1963-2010 series, so its factual content is not in dispute; the implication for regime detection is that a volatility-normalised tail-shape measure is, by construction, blind to volatility-regime onsets. Claim [16] 3-0, verifier confidence high.

Sources: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19375/w19375.pdf

### A daily cross-sectional Hill tail index built from pooled 5-minute returns on 100 S&P 500 stocks (Almeida, Freire, Garcia & Hizmeri, working paper Dec 2022) predicts next-period S&P 500 volatility out of sample at 1-, 5- and 22-day horizons - the only surviving evidence of OOS skill at a days-to-weeks horizon for a volatility-regime target - but with a NEGATIVE sign (fatter cross-sectional left tail -> lower future VIX^2 and realized variance) and modest magnitude: with a rolling 500-day window and Campbell-Thompson OOS R2 vs the historical mean, the risk-neutralised index lambda^Q gives OOS R2 for log VIX^2 of 6.19%/5.98%/4.56% (h=1/5/22), for log realized variance 1.86%/2.13%/0.92%, and for the variance risk premium 4.22%/3.43%/1.37%; the physical index lambda^P is weaker (1.90/1.67/1.04% for VIX^2) and WORSE than the historical mean for realized variance (-0.64/-0.80/-1.44%). In-sample Newey-West t-stats on lambda^Q for VIX^2 are -13.7/-12.1/-9.4.

Confidence: medium; vote 3-0 (claim [19])

Verified against the Lancaster Financial Econometrics Conference 2023 PDF ('Tail risk and asset prices in the short-term', draft 13 Dec 2022). Data: TickData 5-minute returns for 100 stocks in the S&P 500 throughout 2000-2020, 78 intraday obs x 100 stocks = 7,800 pooled observations per day; Hill estimator (eq. 2) with threshold at the 5th percentile of the pooled cross-section; 'The higher the lambda_t, the fatter the stock returns' left tails', and the authors describe 'an inverse relation between activity in the left tail and volatility'. Protocol: 'The out-of-sample exercise is based on a rolling window of 500 days', R2oos = 1 - sum(F - Fhat)^2 / sum(F - Fbar)^2 with Fbar the historical mean through t (Campbell & Thompson 2008); dependent variables are log variances; Newey-West lag = h. Table 3 to rounding: log VIX2 lambda^Q 6.185/5.975/4.561, lambda^P 1.900/1.672/1.042; log RV lambda^Q 1.862/2.126/0.921, lambda^P -0.636/-0.802/-1.442; VRP lambda^Q 4.217/3.429/1.367; in-sample t on lambda^Q for VIX2 -13.675/-12.095/-9.398. Quote: 'the predictive power of the risk-neutral tail measure consistently dominates that of the physical one, in- and out-of-sample.' Limitations: unpublished working paper (publication status unconfirmed); Table 3 reports no Clark-West/Diebold-Mariano test of OOS significance, only in-sample stars; OOS R2 of 1-6% is modest; the target is the LEVEL of future variance (a negative-sign vol predictor), not drawdown or crash onset; a single source, so confidence is capped at medium despite verbatim verification and a 3-0 vote.

### The rolling-window GARCH-alpha-stable crash-forecasting model of Molina-Munoz, Mora-Valencia & Perote (Physica A 2020) produces only a marginal early-warning signal in its own subprime illustration: the forecast probability of an S&P 500 return at or below the 2.5% quantile rose from a full-sample average of 5.95% to 6.09%, 6.14% and 6.18% at 30, 15 and 7 days before the -9.2% drop of 29 Sep 2008 (and from 0.46% to 0.55-0.60% for a >=5% daily drop), i.e. roughly a 0.2 percentage-point rise, and the authors concede 'the severity of the crash was not anticipated'.
